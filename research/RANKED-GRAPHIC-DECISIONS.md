# Ranked graphic-design decisions

From **36 YouTube videos** (transcripts + keyframes) and **35 articles** on posters, social graphics, packaging, branding, print and editorial design: 1,514 decisions clustered into 659 and ranked by **independent creators** (51 creators; a channel with 9 videos counts once). Grounding spot-checks against transcripts: 156 of 169 supported; unsupported claims removed.

> **Important:** in three blind tests (24 judge votes), designs made by following these rules as a skill lost to designs made with no guidance (3 of 24 votes). Use this as a reference for critique and learning, not as a step-by-step recipe. The strongest predictor of a winning graphic was one concept built from the brief's own facts.

## Where sources disagree

- **Some rules ask for generous negative space (about 60% empty, never scale text up to fill gaps). Others ask for a headline across 80-100% of the width, a giant letter cropped off the edge, a full-bleed image, a product covering half the canvas or more, and elements bleeding off the trim.** — This is not a real contradiction once the hook is separated from everything else. By default, exactly one element may be enormous or full-bleed. Every other element stays small and grouped, with unfilled space around it. Nothing is ever added or enlarged just to fill a gap, so 'fill the format' applies to one element, never to the number of elements. Set the proportions by price tier and viewing distance. Premium, luxury, cultural and Swiss pieces: hero at roughly 40% of the area or less, about 60% empty, quiet small type. Street posters, OOH, social thumbnails and promotions: hero type at 80-100% of the width or a full-bleed image, but still no secondary decoration. A full-bleed photo counts as negative space where the area behind the type is calm (sky, wall, shadow), because the empty space moves into the image. Whitespace must always have a reason: grouping, isolating the hook or signalling the tier. Dense information formats (timetables, catalogues, newspapers) legitimately run tight. Fix cramped content by cutting copy or splitting it across slides, never by shrinking margins.
- **One rule wants an equal margin frame with all text and logos inside it and key information near the centre to survive crops. Others want bleed off the trim, a giant word cropped by the edge, a cut-out breaking its box, or a visible border frame.** — The margin frame and safe zone win by default. Every word someone has to read and the logo sit inside it. On social, the safe zone also allows for platform UI and grid-preview crops (4:5 shown as 1:1, the top and bottom bands on Stories). Key information must be inside that zone, though not necessarily centred. Allowed exceptions, one per piece: (a) photos and background fields bleed freely, with print bleed or a dieline offset; (b) one display word or numeral may run off the edge, provided the word stays recognisable, its first letter is never cut, and no more than about the outer quarter is lost; (c) one cut-out may break its image box. A visible border frame is an alternative to bleed, not an addition. Use a frame to contain busy illustrated or cultural pieces or to unify a series, use bleed for impact, and never use both on one piece.
- **Some rules say to snap every edge to whole grid modules and never eyeball. Others say treat the grid as a framework rather than a straitjacket, let elements sit near guides, break the grid, push one element past correct, and vary compositions.** — The strict grid wins. Every text block and image edge sits on a module boundary, and sizes relate in simple ratios. That precision is what separates designed work from generated work. Adjusting by eye is limited to optical corrections: overshoot on round letters, hanging punctuation, pulling large display type left so the stem aligns rather than the sidebearing, and visually centring a cut-out whose mass is uneven. Then add exactly one deliberate break at the key message (an oversized word, a rotated shape, a cut-out over the headline). A break only reads as intentional against a strict grid. In a series, flexibility means varying which modules are filled, not loosening alignment. Golden-section or 1:1.414 proportions are where you start, but the final values are still whole modules. Loose placement near the guides is acceptable only on gestural, hand-made cultural posters. Corporate, editorial, wayfinding and packaging info panels get no breaks at all.
- **One rule says that when you centre, centre exactly on the axis. Others call centred, symmetric layouts the generic AI default and prefer flush-left power lines and rule-of-thirds placement. A further rule says to keep key information centred for platform crops.** — Asymmetric, flush-left layout on one or two power lines is the default for posters, editorial, corporate, social and most back panels. Centre the whole composition, exactly, on evenly spaced guides, only in these cases: ceremonial pieces (invitations, certificates, fine-dining menus, business cards); monumental luxury or heritage work where a frontal product or figure stands on the axis (fragrance, spirits labels, jewellery); symmetric containers (bottle, jar and can fronts); single-figure film one-sheets; circular or avatar crops. Never mix centred and flush-left within one text group. A shared left axis is good structure. The AI tell is uniform alignment combined with uniform weight. Fix that with scale contrast and one counterweight, not by scattering alignments. Crop-safety means staying inside the safe zone, not centring.
- **Some rules say use 1-2 families, never 3, never mix scripts or switch fonts word by word. Others recommend a script or serif word locked up with a sans, hand lettering, and contrasting display and text faces.** — The default is one family, with hierarchy built from size and weight. This suits Swiss, corporate, luxury and editorial interiors. Add a second family only when the roles differ (display vs text) or when the class contrast carries meaning, and treat three as a hard ceiling (for example editorial with a mono for data). The two-font headline lockup counts as the second family, not an extra one. It appears once per piece as a single unit and suits café, food, beauty, lifestyle and seasonal social. Keep it out of finance, B2B and tech. Hand lettering replaces the display face and stays on one element when the rest has to look polished. A heavy pivot word in the same family is the cleaner alternative to a lockup: use one technique per headline, never both. Mixing many faces is allowed only when the vernacular itself is the concept (punk zine, wood-type specimen, protest bill) and the whole piece commits to it.
- **One rule says never pair sans with sans and to combine different classifications. Other rules praise a heavy condensed display sans over small Helvetica, or a single neutral grotesk superfamily.** — The real rule is that two faces must be either clearly different or the same family, never nearly alike. Sans plus sans works when the contrast in width and weight is extreme and the roles differ, for example an ultra-condensed black display over a regular grotesk at text size. It fails when two similar grotesks or geometrics sit at similar sizes. For clean work the default is one superfamily with many weights and widths (Univers, Helvetica Now, Inter), where width and weight supply the contrast. Use serif plus sans when the tone needs warmth or authority (editorial, cultural, premium consumer).
- **One side says 'type shouldn't bark': no stretching, warping or novelty faces that illustrate the word. The other says title type should carry the tone (the Jaws hook J, italics for speed), stretch letterforms to their limit and crop them.** — Neutral setting wins for all informational text: body copy, dates, prices, details, wayfinding and pack information. One display element (a film or event title, wordmark or product name) may carry tone. Do it with drawn or purpose-made letterforms, true condensed or extended cuts, or deliberate scale, crop and tight kerning. Never do it with themed novelty fonts, software horizontal scaling, outlines, bevels or effects. The test is that the word still reads in one glance at its viewing distance. Expressive display type belongs in film, music, events, fashion, packaging names and logos, and stays out of annual reports, finance, healthcare and public information.
- **Some rules make size the first lever: 2x minimum, 3-6x for drama, extreme weight jumps, the most grid area for the key element. Others set all type at one size and build hierarchy from weight and line spaces, or emphasise through isolating white space rather than making things bigger, bolder or a new colour.** — Size-led hierarchy is the default for anything seen at distance or in a scroll (posters, OOH, social, pack fronts): at least 2x per step, 3-6x for the display jump, and weights at least one step apart. Single-size hierarchy (bold plus spacing) is correct for close reading: editorial subheads, programmes, credit blocks, menus, stationery, Swiss information posters and reports. In premium and luxury work, emphasis through isolation is the signature, because loud size and colour read as cheap. Use one restrained weight and let space and placement do the ranking. The best combination is one big jump between the display element and everything else, then one size within the secondary and body tier, with differences in weight and space.
- **Some rules limit each piece to 2-3 colours on a neutral base with one sparing accent. Others want flat three-colour saturated blocking, stark black/yellow pairs, vibrant colour for celebratory work, avoiding dainty category neutrals, and colour-coding many groups or variants.** — The default is a neutral base (off-white, warm grey, near-black) with one accent that has a fixed job. This wins for premium, luxury, corporate, editorial and healthcare work. Use flat saturated blocking in no more than three colours, with no gradients, when the brand is outgoing, youthful or playful, for event and music posters and children's or family products, or when the category is a sea of neutrals and you need to stand out. Use stark value and hue pairs where distance and thumbnail legibility rule: OOH, sales, sport, YouTube thumbnails, never luxury. Colour-coding beyond three colours is fine when colour is the information system (variants, activities, transit lines). Each single piece or pack still shows the base plus its own code colour, the codes come from one documented extended palette at equal saturation, and everything else stays neutral.
- **One rule puts a single accent only on the element the eye must land on. Others repeat that colour on the logo, icons, headings and CTA, use 1-2 accents for the top levels, or impose a mandated signature brand colour.** — On a single poster or post the accent belongs to the hook, plus at most one echo at the end of the reading path (the CTA or logo) so the eye loops back. Repeating it across headings, icons and section openers belongs in multi-page systems (sites, brochures, decks, editorial), where the colour marks a category rather than a focal point. If the signature brand colour is used as a large field, the focal accent must be a different colour or a neutral, so the two do not compete.
- **Rules variously say: let a striking image dominate; desaturate or knock back the image; grade photos toward the brand palette; build the palette from the photo; make the focal element the complement of its surroundings; apply one global grade.** — First decide whether the image is the hook or the support. If it is the hook, keep it at full strength. For one-off cultural or editorial pieces, derive or bias the page and type colours from the image. For brand work, grade the image toward the fixed brand palette: whenever a brand system exists, the brand palette beats colours taken from the photo. If the image is support, knock it back with a multiply tint, monochrome or lower contrast so the type leads. The complementary-focal technique is a grading move inside the image (push the surroundings toward the complement of the subject), not permission to add a new palette colour. Keep global grades subtle and plausible: no teal-orange, golden-hour or HDR-glow presets.
- **Photo-led ('one striking image dominates', 'product as hero', 'don't rely on type alone') versus type-led ('get everything out of type first', 'headline as the image', 'message as hook').** — Never split the lead 50/50. Pick one route. Go image-led when a real image carries the message on its own: film, people and emotion, travel, fashion. Go product-led when you are selling a physical product and have proper photography (product largest, tight crop, short copy). Go type-led when the message is information or an idea (events with names and dates, cultural programmes, offers, announcements, quotes, B2B) or when the only imagery available is generic stock or AI. Pure typography beats visual elevator music. Adding one image (imagery_media.0.9) applies only when a type-only consumer piece feels cold and a specific, owned image exists. It never justifies stock. Film and event key art can be reduced to one flat figure with a small bold title.
- **Sober, factual photography for credibility versus hand-drawn, imperfect illustration for humanity, flat 2-3 colour key art, and hand-drawn marks over clean layouts. Separately, one rule says lock a single visual treatment with no stray styles.** — Photography wins where credibility, appetite appeal or product truth matter: finance, health, property, journalism, food, technical products, fashion. Illustration wins where there is nothing to photograph (software, services, abstract ideas), where a handmade, human tone is the brand point (craft food, community, small business), and for reductive film or event key art. Hand-drawn marks over typeset layouts are allowed only as a consistent system layer (same tool, colour and stroke on every piece) in casual consumer or community work, never in corporate, legal or medical work. Never mix illustration styles or drop 3D or gradient assets into a flat set.
- **Some rules favour imperfection: personality over flawless 'mid' work, controlled imperfection, grain and paper texture, riso misregistration. Others demand precision: snap alignment, hand-kern, sharp hi-res images, a flat locked palette.** — Structure is always precise. Alignment, spacing, kerning, resolution and legibility are never where personality goes. Imperfection lives in a single surface layer: scanned real-material texture, lettering, a riso or letterpress look, or a gestural crop. That layer suits craft, food, music, culture and print-vernacular pieces. It is wrong for fintech, healthcare and B2B SaaS, and usually for luxury, where crisp flatness or real stock is the signal. In physical print, use the actual uncoated or textured stock rather than simulated grain. On screen, set textures from real scans to Screen or Multiply at low opacity, subtle enough to go unnoticed at a glance. Fix 'flawless but mid' with a stronger concept, not by adding noise.
- **One side says every element must have a nameable job, to remove meaningless decoration and to group with space rather than dividers. The other adds arrows, dotted lines, chevrons, rule hierarchies, solid bars behind offers, visible frames and logo-derived background motifs.** — Removal wins by default. For directional bridges, prefer ones already in the content (gaze, gesture, the subject's line, the product's angle, a shared alignment). Add explicit arrows or dotted lines only for instructions, wayfinding, maps or 'we moved' information. Rules are structural tools for dense Swiss, editorial and form layouts (heavy between parts, hairline between items, type hanging from them). In sparse posters, space and colour fields do the grouping. Bars behind secondary hooks are for promotional work, to lock the offer to the headline. Background motifs are allowed only when derived from the logo or concept and kept faint. Use at most 1-2 small graphic marks per piece, each with a job you can name.
- **Some rules encourage layering: overlap the title with the subject, put the head or bottle in front of the letters, fuse type into the figure. Others forbid setting type over faces or focal points and burying type, and require 4.5:1 contrast.** — Overlap is for display type only. Use it only on the largest word, which survives partial occlusion: hide no more than about a quarter to a third of a few letters, and the word must still read at a glance. Put the subject in front. Never overlap body copy, dates, prices or anything needed to act, and never run type across eyes, mouths or product labels. Allow one overlap per piece. Readable text keeps 4.5:1 contrast. Over a photo, edit the photo (levels, a darkened area) rather than lowering the opacity of the type. Fusing type into the figure (Bass) is the premium version of this, and it must come from the concept, not from a layer effect.
- **Minimal, clean, function-first restraint versus leading with a point of view, being 'a bit weird', and rejecting polished, safe, characterless output.** — Restraint governs how many elements there are. Boldness governs the idea. A minimal piece with no idea (default grotesk, centred, safe stock) is itself an AI tell. The default is few elements plus one strong conceptual move: an unexpected crop, extreme scale, a double reading, or suggesting rather than depicting. For public information (signage, transit, forms, medical), clarity beats weirdness. For cultural, fashion, music and self-initiated work, the metaphorical idea leads but is still executed with full craft.
- **One rule says the headliner must state what the piece is and who it is for, and convey the message in a one-second glance. Others say suggest rather than show, leave the viewer something to resolve, and give one image two readings.** — The commercial and informational default is that the dominant element, or the text directly beside it, states the what (and the offer). Suggestive or double-reading hooks are fine for film, culture, campaigns by already-known brands and editorial covers. The name must then sit next to the hook at secondary size and read within the one-second glance, and the first reading of a double image must be instant. Never use a suggestive hook on sales, recruitment, public health or wayfinding.
- **Some rules say build on timeless fundamentals and avoid fashionable looks. Others say borrow a specific era or vernacular, name a style, mood-board real references, and read category defaults then go with or against them.** — Avoid current trends by default (glass, mesh gradients, Gen Z more-is-more, startup geometric minimalism). That is absolute for logos, identities and packaging systems expected to last 5+ years. Borrowing one specific, nameable historical vernacular (1960s Swiss, Sachplakat, riso fanzine, transit wayfinding) is allowed for campaigns, events, limited editions and social, but only when it relates to the subject and is executed precisely, not as a filter. Build mood boards from real references across eras, not this month's Dribbble. With category conventions, keep the functional codes shoppers navigate by (flavour or variant colours, format signals, legal zones) and break the aesthetic defaults. Challengers break more, leaders and heritage brands less.
- **Some rules say use Helvetica, Akzidenz or Univers and don't reject them for being overused, choose classic families, and simplify trust identities to Swiss crispness. Others say don't reduce an identity to a plain grotesk wordmark anyone could own (Helvetica named as a cliché), and make the wordmark ownable.** — Neutral grotesks and classics are right for the communication layer: layout text, Swiss posters, documents, campaign typography. The identity's distinguishing asset must never be an unmodified grotesk. Customise it (spacing, letter details, a custom cut), pair it with an ownable symbol or colour, or pick a face that runs against the category. Trust identities can stay crisp and Swiss but need at least one ownable cue. The test: cover the name and check whether an asset still points to this brand alone.
- **The personality mappings (serif for trust, rounded for kids, bold sans for tech or sport) push toward expected category choices. Other rules say go against the category default, don't pick a look because it is fashionable, use only proven classics and avoid novelty faces.** — The personality mapping decides which signals the type must send (trust, warmth, energy). The category audit decides whether to send them through the expected class or an unexpected one. In regulated or trust-critical categories (banks, health, legal), match the expected signals and distinguish through custom details. In crowded or challenger categories (DTC, fintech, beverages), choose against the category default as long as the personality still comes through (for example a warm serif for a fintech that wants to feel human). Take the specific face from well-made families with full weight ranges. Rounded or 'fun' faces belong in children's and family brands, as display type only.
- **Lock everything (identical sizes, palette, image treatment, functional zones on every piece) versus consistency without sameness: never clone compositions, vary layouts, choose a colour per emotion, and build a variable system around one anchor.** — Fix the identifiers: logo and clearspace, type families and type scale, core palette, image treatment, margins and grid, and functional zones across a packaging range. Vary the composition within the grid, the crops, which modules are filled, and which palette colour dominates. A mood-driven subset of the fixed palette is fine. In carousels and series, keep the headline size and position identical on every slide and vary the layouts below. Never introduce off-palette colours or new faces per piece. A seasonal or limited edition may add one guest colour and must keep everything else.
- **Evolve an equity-rich identity by keeping its core, versus designing deliberately against the category, pushing toward original 'weird' ideas, and committing to one concept even to a theatrical or humorous edge.** — Brands with recognisable assets evolve: keep the core shape, colour or quirk, modernise the execution, and put the originality into the system around that anchor. New brands, events and one-off campaigns start against the category from one organising idea. Full theatrical commitment suits DTC and challenger brands whose audiences reward wit. Even then, build the identifiers themselves (mark, palette, type) on fundamentals and keep the theatre in copy, imagery and materials, so the joke doesn't date the brand.
- **One set of rules keeps the logo small and subordinate in a corner. Another gives the brand zone the upper ~60% of a pack and a mandated brand-colour block on every front panel.** — In ads, posters, social and promotions, the logo is small in a corner or band and never outranks the headline or product. Luxury ads show the product huge and the wordmark tiny. On pack front panels for established brands and repeat-purchase categories, the brand block leads at shelf distance and the product or variant comes second. For a new product, private label or innovation, where the benefit is the reason to buy, the product name or visual may lead and the brand sits second. Profile images and app icons are the one place where the mark is the whole piece.
- **Dramatic ratios (body down to 1/15 of the heading, small light type next to a huge display) and 'never fix layouts by scaling text up' clash with legibility floors: 48/32/24 pt minimums on social, sturdy faces for distance, and 'if it can't be read in passing, increase size'.** — Legibility floors beat ratios. The ratio sets the gap, the medium sets the floor. At 1080 px on social nothing goes below about 24 pt and headlines are 48 pt or more. On posters the smallest tier must read at arm's length and the headline at walk-past distance. Print body text is sized to its measure. Enlarging text for legibility is correct. Enlarging it to fill space is not. If the ratio and the floor can't both fit, cut copy or move it to a caption or landing page rather than going below the floor.
- **Bold, sturdy faces for distance, glances and busy photos, versus thin, light and script faces for elegance, luxury restraint and pairing small light type with a big display.** — Viewing distance and background decide the weight. OOH, transit, shelf and social over photos need bold, sturdy faces. Thin, hairline, light and script faces work only when viewed up close (magazines, packs held in the hand, stationery) or at very large sizes on a clean ground. On social, add 8-12 pt to them. Luxury out-of-home keeps its elegance through scale and space and a sturdier display cut, not hairlines at small sizes.
- **Some rules mute secondary content: muted tones for secondary text, background images at low opacity, a tone-on-tone logo. Others require 4.5:1 contrast for all text and checking value contrast in greyscale.** — Make things recede through size, weight and position, never by dropping below the contrast floor. Every word a viewer needs keeps 4.5:1 (check in greyscale). Muting, low opacity and tone-on-tone are only for non-informational layers: background imagery, texture, pattern, or a decorative logo repeat when a legible logo also appears. To knock back a photo, prefer a value or colour treatment (multiply tint, monochrome, levels) over plain opacity, which washes it into grey haze.
- **Signal the price tier: discount means big, bold, crowded and premium means whitespace. Against that: one focal element and cutting copy to essentials, and deadpan luxury presentation of humble products.** — Show value through loudness, not clutter: big bold type, high contrast, the price or offer as the hook, tighter margins, but still one focal element and a cut-down message. Density is legitimate only where many items genuinely exist (circulars, product grids, menus), and then it sits on a strict grid. Premium work gets whitespace, restraint and mood-led copy. Deliberate high-low (luxury seriousness for a humble product) is a concept for fashion and DTC brands whose audiences are fluent in irony. By default, signal the tier honestly.
- **Several rules claim to come first: draw the grid before placing anything; list the content and settle the layout idea before choosing the grid; sketch thumbnails before software; write copy before style; build a mood board before committing.** — The order is: the brief and one central idea, then the real copy, then the content inventory and format, then a reference board, then 3-4 paper thumbnail directions. Derive the grid from the content density and the chosen layout, then block out in greyscale on the grid, then set type, then add imagery, colour and texture. 'Grid before anything' means before placing anything in software, not before thinking. Never reuse a default grid across projects.
- **Rule-of-thirds placement, power lines, whole modules, one explicit ratio, rotated diagonal grids, angled bands and diagonal format splits all compete as the governing system.** — The module grid governs. The rule of thirds is only a crop guide for photos. Choose a column count (3, 6, 9 or 12) whose lines coincide with the thirds so the two agree, and let the type still hang on grid lines. Use one ratio throughout, ideally the paper's own. Rotated grids, diagonal splits and angled bands are only for subjects about energy or motion (sport, music, speed products, protest), with one angle per piece and an orthogonal column grid underneath. Never use them for luxury, finance or healthcare.
- **Lock all text, headlines included, to one baseline grid in whole multiples of the body leading, versus giant display type at 5-15x body, set tight and cropped.** — The baseline grid governs editorial and multi-column text: body, captions, subheads and spacing on multiples. Giant poster display type aligns its cap height or baseline to a row or module line instead, with leading set tight by eye (often under 100%). Don't force display lines into body-leading increments.
- **Cut copy to the minimum needed to act and give each post one topic, versus letting long-dwell placements carry more text.** — Cut hard in glance formats (billboards, thumbnails, feed posts, pack fronts): a 1-7 word headline and only the essentials. Long-dwell placements (transit, bus shelters, gallery walls, programme posters, pack back panels, editorial) may carry more text, but only in the smallest tier, in a column with a controlled measure, and with the hook still dominant. A multi-topic brief becomes several posts or slides, never one dense post.
- **Cut the hero out onto stark white or flat colour, versus a full-bleed image that keeps its environment.** — Use a cut-out on flat colour when the background is noise and the subject has to layer with type (product, athlete, fashion, sale). It needs clean hair masks, consistent lighting and a real contact shadow, or it reads as a Canva sticker. Use full-bleed environmental photography when place or atmosphere is the message (travel, film, food in context, documentary). For premium products, shoot specifically for the cut-out rather than extracting one from stock.
- **Define an owned imagery style instead of stock, versus 'use reputable stock' and striking cinematic imagery. AI-tell rules also warn against generic stock, attractor subjects and default spectacle.** — Any brand with ongoing output needs owned, art-directed imagery. Stock is acceptable for one-offs and small budgets, provided it is distinctive (abstract, texture, archival, documentary), avoids attractor subjects and spectacle lighting unless the concept needs them, is regraded to the palette, is treated consistently across the set and is high resolution. If neither owned nor distinctive imagery exists, go type-led.
- **Some rules describe how to steer AI generation (name a style, write the art direction, generate text-free imagery and set type live). Others say don't publish AI output, make the piece by hand, prefer hand-drawn over polished AI, never generate textures, and keep AI posters out of creative institutions.** — For premium work the default is real photography, illustration, scanned textures and live type, with AI limited to grunt work (masking, upscaling, background extension, vectorising, exploration thumbnails). AI hero imagery is acceptable only when all of these hold: a human writes the art direction and names a specific style; a batch is generated and audited for bias, physics errors and gibberish; the default first output is discarded; the result is regraded; and all text is set live. It is never acceptable for libraries, schools, cultural institutions or brands whose promise is human-made work. Never use it for textures (scan real material), and never use images of people without human review.
- **One rule says one dominant hook and nothing competing at equal weight. Another says repetition (a repeated title, outline-word patterns) can replace a single focal point.** — A single hook is the default. Repetition can replace it only when the repeated pattern reads as one image at a distance (a title repeated as a border, a pattern on a pack lid, music or cultural posters, brand wallpaper) and the information on top keeps a clear hierarchy. Avoid it on informational and sales pieces.
- **Gloss and metallic foil read as high-end and opulent and kraft reads natural, versus restrained, matte, modern premium. Kraft is also flagged as a category default to avoid, and gold or gloss effects are flagged as AI tells on screen.** — For modern premium, editorial, minimal and stationery work, use matte, uncoated or cotton stock: tactile quality is the luxury signal. Use gloss or foil only for opulent categories (fragrance, jewellery, gifting confectionery, cosmetics) or for high-saturation photography, and preferably as a spot (foil or spot UV on the mark) rather than a flood. Real foil on paper is legitimate, but simulated gold text on screen is an AI tell. Use kraft only when 'natural' is true of the product and the brand isn't trying to stand out in a kraft-saturated category.
- **Fill gaps in the brief yourself instead of waiting, versus never acting on vague adjectives and breaking them into concrete parameters with the client first.** — Fill factual gaps yourself: research, draft copy from the topic list, find specs and dielines. Never fill aesthetic meaning gaps yourself. Turn 'clean', 'elegant' or 'epic' into named parameters or reference images agreed with the client before designing. Push back on briefs that arrive with the visual answer already decided, without stalling the work.
