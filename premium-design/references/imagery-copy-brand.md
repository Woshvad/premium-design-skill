# Imagery, copy and brand

`[N]` = number of independent creators who state the rule (a channel with several videos counts once). `[1★]` = one canonical source (Apple HIG, Butterick, Vercel, Rauno, Kowalski, Anthropic, NN/g, Hobday, Kennedy, Linear) or a single source that fills a real gap. `(std)` = widely accepted standard, not taken from the research. Method, backers and source index: [evidence.md](evidence.md). Palette, gradient and grain values: [color.md](color.md).

**Summary of the highest-weight rules**
- Before styling, write down the brand's personality, its market position and the 1-3 emotions users should feel. Derive type, colour, imagery and motion from that [8]. Keep one design language on every section and touchpoint [7].
- Show the real thing. Use isolated product cut-outs on a sweep that matches the section surface [7], real UI and real outputs instead of orbs and glows [6], and a few commissioned images graded as one set [6][6]. Generic stock and placeholder art read as AI [3].
- The first viewport says what the product is, who it is for and what it achieves, with the headline carrying one of the three in the brand's own words. It must be specific enough that no competitor could reuse it, fit in 2 lines or fewer, and sit with one CTA that names its action [7].
- Every visible string is real and specific: no lorem ipsum, no buzzwords, no invented stats, and buttons labelled with their action [4][4][3]. Then run the swap test on every section [4].
- Defaults are a floor, not a house style. For each brief you still decide the emotion, light or dark, sharp or round, sans or serif display, the accent hue, the sweep colour, isolated or in-use photography, and a witty or plain voice.

---

## 1. Define the brand before any styling

- **Personality first** [8]. Before choosing any value, write down the personality, the market position and 1-3 target emotions (calm, confident, warm, excited). Choose every layer to express them: overall style, typeface, palette, shape and material, illustration, motion easing and duration. Don't fall back on "minimal and sleek" when the feeling calls for warmth. Headspace uses soft colour, rounded type and gentle motion. Sonner deliberately runs slower with `ease` to feel elegant.
- **One-sentence design thesis** [6]. Write one core emotion or idea, plus page kind, audience and vibe: "Reading this as: <page kind> for <audience>, with a <vibe> language". It holds one emotion, not five. Audit every choice against it, and ask at most one clarifying question.
- **Anchor the look in a concrete reference from the subject's world** [6]. That can be an object, an era, the industry's own vernacular or a premium player in the niche. State it as positive art direction, never as adjectives. A toy for 8-11-year-olds must not look like an analyst dashboard. One such sentence carries more than a dozen metric values. Choose the layer of that world the audience desires and that carries the target emotions (the coveted object, the place at its best, the craft's finest moment), not its paperwork or instruments: rubber stamps, batch records, gauges, cockpit faces and Gantt charts import an administrative feel unless the emotion is precision. Every word of the product's own promise ("smoky", "warm", "a way back") must be visible in the first viewport, not only written in it (synthesis).
- **Lock the system as exact values before laying out pages** [5]. Fix the hex values for base, accent and chart colours, the heading and body fonts, the type scale, the spacing scale, radius, elevation, motion and icon library. Keep them in one spec file (DESIGN.md, a theme preset or tokens) that every screen and every AI prompt reads from. If there is a dark mode, check the theme in both modes.
- **Moodboard that world before wireframing the hero** [4]. Examples: film and studio grammar for a video tool, print editorial for a publication, cartography or archives. Then specify its palette, type, borders, textures, shapes and lighting, and apply them to every screen. Name colour tokens after that world [4].
- **Write the approved look into a style guide** [3]: palette, type, spacing, layout, media, motion, buttons, nav, mobile behaviour, and how new sections extend it. Build every later screen from it.
- **Feed your brand into the AI, never accept its house look** [3]. Give the tool your palette, type and references as input. That includes refusing the vendor's own beige, off-red and big-serif style.
- **Roundness follows tone** [3]. Choose sharp (0-4px) or soft corners from the reference world (the product's hardware, packaging, signage, the brand mark), not from the category label: sharp tends to read luxury or editorial, soft reads friendly. Use rounded letterforms (SwiftUI `.font(.system(.body, design: .rounded))`) only for a friendly tone or a soft UI, never by default. Never mix sharp and soft in one view.
- **Choose durable forms over fashion, the logo included** [3]. Brush-stroke blobs and skewed decorative wordmarks date fast. Test: would it still look deliberate in 5 years, and would you pick it if no competitor were doing it?
- **One bold brand accent** [3]. Reuse the client's existing colour if there is one. Spend it on the logo, the primary CTA, active nav and chart series, with at most one supporting band, on a neutral canvas (restrained strategy; a committed strategy may give the brand hue 30-60% of the surface, color.md C3). When simplifying, drop the effects (gradients) and keep the colour. Decide per brief: take the hue from the brand's world, not the generator's violet.
- **Pair tokens with prose** [2]. Open the spec with the personality, the audience and the intended emotion. Give each colour an evocative name mapped to its token, then its job and its feeling ("the primary accent; should feel clear and trustworthy").
- **Restraint over show** [2]. Don't style functional products as decorative objects. Put the effort into craft and production quality.
- **Clean by default** [1★] (Kennedy). Attempt a loud multicolour, sporty or cartoony style only when the product needs that attitude and someone on the team handles colour very well.

## 2. Imagery and product photography

- **Isolated cut-outs on a seamless sweep** [7], for product, catalogue and detail views (hero and campaign moments get one art-directed scene per section, and venue or food briefs lead with the place and the plates in situ; C1). Shoot the product centred or at a three-quarter view, with only a soft contact shadow and generous empty space. Show collections as evenly spaced rows or radial fans of colourways, not lifestyle scenes. Match the sweep exactly to the section surface so the cut-out floats with no visible image edge. Apple's light grey is about #F5F5F7. apple.com reportedly uses a single shadow, `rgba(0,0,0,.22) 3px 5px 30px`, and only on product renders. Decide per brief: white, light grey, brand-tinted or black sweep.
- **The real product is the star of each section** [6]. Show actual UI screenshots, real code, real generated outputs or real interior pages, never abstract illustrations, glows or orbs. They can be static, lightly skewed or interactive. Linear shows screenshots in 16px-radius panels across the full content width, never cropped. For software, real UI is the product photography.
- **Few images, each editorial-grade and commissioned** [6]. Art-direct lighting, composition and negative space as carefully as the subject. Ideally use one photographer, and re-shoot rather than mix quality levels. Swap a premium image for a lesser one and the premium effect is gone. Keep video poster frames sharp and matching their video.
- **One visual treatment per page** [6]. Grade every photo with the same tone, light, contrast and framing, and never mix illustration styles (no stray gradient or 3D asset in a flat set). Match the imagery to the UI: soft studio photos for a soft UI, clay illustration for a clay UI. Drop or re-shoot outliers.
- **Real photography in the hero and key sections** [5], not plain colour, gradients, glows or illustration. Even a minimal landing page needs 2-3 real images (hero, product or in-use, supporting); docs and reading pages are text-led and use images and diagrams only where they explain (C7). Test: if the first viewport still works with the image removed, the image is too weak. One decisive photo beats five mediocre ones. Never ship an empty `<img>`, fake photo credits or fake tags on photos.
- **Replace placeholders with the real asset** [3]. Use headshots for testimonial avatars, not initials. Use the actual product photo for a saved-item thumbnail. Use real SVG logos in logo walls (Simple Icons, devicon), not styled text. A logo wall holds logos only, with no category captions.
- **Show the product in real human use** [3], alongside the isolated shots: hands, ears, a desk by a window. Each in-use photo proves one specific feature (a dim room for night mode, a device held edge-on for thinness).
- **Black stage for flagship or pro moments** [3]. Centre the product on black, rim-light it with sharp specular highlights along its edges, and add one line of copy. Rugged products get moody black-and-white shots in extreme environments.
- **Grain only where it adds material** [3]. Use subtle noise on hero photos and flat-colour fields, quiet enough never to compete with the subject. Leave out any section meant to look crisp.
- **Reject distracting detail** [2]: background clutter, bystanders, unretouched skin, over-posing, embedded signage, logos or text, and built-in UI frames. Use several clean images rather than one collage.
- **Choose imagery for the emotion it triggers** [2], in line with the audience's motivation, not as a literal view of the product. Travel photos sell an atmosphere, not a destination.
- **Show how it is made** [2]. Use photos and footage of machining, moulding and test rigs instead of prose about craft.
- **Image sets run horizontally** [2]: a large main image with a thumbnail strip, or a horizontal scroller with consistent gaps, minimal text and the next card peeking off the edge.
- **Clear glass only over rich media** [2]. A highly translucent panel needs a detailed photo or video under it; over a flat colour it just looks transparent.
- **Delivery** [2]. Give every image explicit `width`/`height`. Use `fetchpriority="high"` only above the fold and `loading="lazy"` everywhere else. Use `srcset`/`sizes` (example: 200w/500w/1000w, `(min-width:30em) 25vw, 50vw`) [1★], and `<picture>` for art-directed crops: portrait on small screens, landscape from a breakpoint up [1★]. Decorative images get `alt=""` plus `aria-hidden="true"` [2]. Content images get alt text that describes what the image shows (std).
- **Hardware detail shots** [2] (xrhNIU-BLZw, cdzbM0HABqY): extreme macro crops of edges, ports and joins bleeding off the frame; exploded views settling into the enclosure; thin single-weight technical line overlays on the product. Fill device screens with vivid artwork so the screens supply the page's colour against a neutral ground.
- **Each visual asset at most twice per page, never in adjacent sections** (synthesis). A reused render changes angle or crop; one silhouette carried through three viewports and shown again in the next section is one asset doing four jobs.
- **Drawn in code, when the brief forbids images or none exist yet** (synthesis; the [1★] primitive-SVG tell in §5 still describes the failure). A hand-built product drawing must earn the hero: an object the audience would covet, not a diagram. Take true proportions from the real dimensions, add real label typography and material cues (glass thickness and refraction, wood grain, brushed metal), and set it in a scene that carries the product's promise (smoke, steam, light on a counter). A well-lit front elevation beats a three-quarter view built from CSS boxes or isometric faces, which reads as a toy block; never a flat silhouette. Light it (SVG `feDiffuseLighting`/`feSpecularLighting` (std) or layered gradients from one light source [2]), with a faint material texture (`feTurbulence` at a few percent opacity [3]). Features such as spouts, ports or vents belong to the outline (cut into it or drawn with it), not shapes stuck onto it; the silhouette must read as the right object at 64px. Repeating or concentric patterns (machining rings, brushed grain, knurling) stay faint, about 2% opacity, or they read as a record or a target. Code-drawn diagrams follow one drafting convention (components-details.md §9). Say in the reply that commissioned photography replaces the drawings, and list the shots needed.
- **Software without photography** (synthesis of [6] real UI, [2] no faked previews and [3] real logos): build 2-4 faithful renders of the product's real surfaces (a terminal session with real commands and output, a review or approval screen, a config file, the app view), with real states and arithmetic and the platform's own chrome conventions. They are the product shots; flat tinted boxes standing in for them leave the page with no product. Real partner SVG marks set at one size and one tone also count toward the 2-3 images. An integrations section of grey plain-text names is a footer in the middle of the page. Sample figures inside a product view get one small caption outside the UI chrome ("Illustrative account, sample figures"), once per figure group, never a chip inside every panel.
- **Full-bleed photography belongs to landing and story pages** [1★] (NN/g). Users found large photos impressive once and obstructive when they were looking for information. Inner pages stay information-dense.
- **Keep strongly coloured images out of secondary slots** [1★] (NN/g). They take over the hierarchy. Test templates with realistic images, not curated ones.
- **Contrast complexity** [1★] (Hobday). Put a simple foreground on a complex background or the reverse, never complex on complex.
- **Video hygiene** [1★] (Vercel). Autoplay only muted, non-essential loops, and give any motion longer than 5s a pause, stop or hide control. Replace GIFs with `<video autoplay muted loop playsinline>`. Build gradient fades from background images, not CSS masks, to avoid banding.

## 3. Hero: composition and headline

- **Value-proposition headline** [7]. The first viewport (headline, one supporting line, the visual) communicates what the product is, who it is for and what outcome it delivers, specifically enough that no competitor could reuse it. The headline carries one of the three in the brand's own words; never the template "The <category> for <audience> that <outcome>". Keep it to 2 lines or fewer on desktop, then 1-3 short supporting lines and one CTA whose label names exactly what happens. Keep the headline and CTA above the fold and the text column narrow. Strong: "Get paid on time" with "Send my first invoice". Weak: "Close better deals faster", "Create Games with AI".
- **First-viewport inventory** [5]: the brand, one short headline, one supporting sentence, one CTA group (a primary button plus at most one text link) and one dominant image or video. Who you are and what you do should land within about 3s. Keep stats, schedules, promos, metadata rows and redundant eyebrows out. Exception (synthesis): a local venue's first viewport also carries hours, address and the book/order CTA, with a real photo of the room or the food.
- **The subject is the biggest thing** [4]. Scale the hero subject to about half the canvas or more, let it crop or bleed off an edge, and keep secondary elements small and quiet.
- **Full-bleed scene hero** [4]. An edge-to-edge image (landscape, product scene, place) with a short overlaid headline and a single CTA or text link, usually centred. It is allowed only when the image has a calm area for the text (C3).
- **Reject template hero skeletons, even in a new skin** [4]. Three skeletons are out. (1) The centred SaaS hero: pill eyebrow, two-tone headline, subheadline, pill CTA plus a ghost "Watch demo", then a stat row and an icon grid. (2) Copy left and image right with a badge row. (3) A centred headline over a row of equal cards. Change the structure, not just the styling.
- **Legible text over photos** [3]. Place text on a calm, even-toned area (crop the image to create one), or add a subtle gradient or blur behind it. Over bright or busy content, use a dark dimming layer of about 35%. You can also flip or mask a colourful image so its colour sits away from the headline [2]. Verify ≥4.5:1 for body text and ≥3:1 for large text against the real image [8].
- **Known-product stack** [3] (Apple pattern): an optional short eyebrow, the product name, a one-line tagline, a small starting price, then at most two small CTAs (a filled pill plus a "Learn more" chevron link). Use it only when the visitor already knows the category (C5).
- **Ambient hero video** [3]. Use `autoplay muted loop playsInline` at `min-height: 100vh`, keep the still as `poster`, keep motion subtle, and build the layout around it. Pick clips shot on a backdrop in the page colour, and reject ones shot on white or on busy backgrounds [1★]. Allowed only when the footage is the content (C9).
- **Story chapters** [3]. Below the hero, give each section one subject: a short headline, a small block of text and one image or video. On visual product pages, chapters can be full-bleed image or video sections with a short centred headline of 2-8 words [2]. The copy plays off what is on screen, and the video adds to the copy rather than replacing it [2].
- **CTA label discipline** [2]. Use 1-3 words that never wrap, and the exact same wording for one intent in the nav, hero and footer ("Get in touch", "Let's talk" and "Contact us" on one page fails).
- **The brand must be identifiable in the first screen** [1★]. Hide the nav. If the first screen could belong to another brand, the branding is too weak. Make the product or brand name a hero-level signal, while the nav logo itself stays small.
- **When the H1 fails the competitor test** (synthesis of [7] and C5): the competitor-name substitution needs no web access and is never waived. If the outcome headline reads true for every competitor (they all "save time", all "last a lifetime"), lead with the one mechanism only this product has and move the outcome to the subline. Before shipping, check that no section H2 is more ownable than the H1; if one is, promote it.
- **Launch pages** (synthesis): name the product in the first viewport at display size (not only inside grey lead text or a small label over a sentence H1; the [5] oversized-display rule lets the name itself be the hero visual, with the specific line under it as a lead), and signal newness once with a date or an availability line beside the CTA. A plain product-name or date label above the H1 is the eyebrow rule's own exception [7]; a "New" pill is not.

## 4. One language across touchpoints

- **One design language everywhere** [7]. Fonts, icons, button style and photo treatment stay identical on every section. Carry the same system to every touchpoint (product family, print, social posts, signage) and every UI scale. Evolve the language over time. Don't replace it with an unrelated look every year.
- **Same identity, platform-tuned variants** [4]. Share palette, type voice, imagery treatment and tone. Keep each platform's native controls, navigation and conventions instead of copying another platform's (C12).
- **Write a few explicit visual rules and apply them to every asset** [3]: a fixed palette of about 4 colours, a few signature shapes, set highlight and shadow hues, and motion, icon and type rules per medium. Such rules make mismatched content (album art, user photos, partner images) read as one system.
- **Give editors written content and photography criteria** [2], plus CMS onboarding, so what they add after handoff keeps the quality.
- **The mark sits quietly in the surface** [2]. Never make it glowing, illuminated or stuck on top. On the web, render it flat in one colour (black, white or the brand colour), with no glow and no gradient.
- **Digital chrome continues the physical product** [2], for hardware brands: match its finish and corner radius, and update the UI skin when the hardware language changes.
- **Survive the next medium** [1★]. Before calling an identity done, check that the logo reads at favicon size (16-32px). Check that it can be embroidered (no fine detail, no gradients), that it animates, and that it still feels like the same brand on a social post, a deck slide and a product mockup.
- **Series: lock two variables** [1★]. In a multi-piece series, fix exactly two things (for example type position and a shared accent) and let subject, crop, layout and type style vary. Consistency comes from shared behaviour, not identical pieces.
- **Your own logo as inline SVG** [1★]. It stays crisp and can take CSS. Partner and customer logos stay images.
- **No logo supplied** (synthesis): derive the wordmark from the signature move (a stamped or engraved mark, a ruled line, a plotted trace) and use it in the header and the favicon. A product-category pictogram beside the name in the text face is a placeholder, and a wordmark treatment must never match an interactive affordance (an underline reads as a link).
- **Luxury marks** [1★], for fashion and luxury briefs only: a wordmark in a clean, often thin serif, set in caps, with no icon, in black or white (white over dark imagery). One option, not the default: combined with B&W chrome, a square black CTA and a centred product on a grey sweep it becomes the category template (SKILL.md section 6).

## 5. AI tells in copy and imagery

Copy:
- **Eyebrows and launch labels** [7]. Drop "Introducing [Product]", "Powered by next-gen AI" and date pills above the H1, and work the words into the heading. Keep at most one eyebrow per three sections, and only when it carries information the headline doesn't.
- **Buzzwords and fake specifics** [4]. Cut streamline, empower, supercharge, world-class, next-generation, and the "Not X. Just Y." cadence. Cut placeholder names and brands (John Doe, Acme, Nexus, SmartFlow) and suspiciously round metrics (99.99%). Never invent precision (92%, 4.1x) unless it comes from real data or is labelled as mock. Name what the product literally does.
- **Fake sequence numbering** [4]. Use 01/02/03, "Step 1" or "SECTION 01" only for a real sequence. Name the topic plainly, and use verb-nouns for steps (Install, Configure, Ship).
- **Re-read every visible string** [3]. Delete Elevate, Seamless, Unleash, Next-Gen, Revolutionize, Game-changer, Delve, Tapestry. Delete cute performative labels: use "Latest writing", not "Field notes", and "Testimonials", not "Quietly in use at". Delete any design commentary or prompt language left in the UI. Keep one register per page. Deletion test: if cutting 30% of the copy improves the page, keep cutting.
- **No em-dashes in visible UI** [3]. Replace them with a period, comma, colon or parentheses, use a hyphen for ranges (2018-2026), and never build labels as "WORD — fragment". One detector flags ≥8 em-dashes at about 1 per 500 characters.
- **No big-number hero template** [3]. A large figure with a small label and a row of vanity metrics (99.9% / 12ms / 40k+) needs real data behind it, or it goes.
- **Separator chains** [2]. Use at most one middle dot or pipe per line ("A · B · C" is a tell).
- **No → arrow appended to every link and button** [1★] (Anthropic).
- **Naming and hero formulas** [1★]. Avoid product names with -ly or -ify or a bolted-on "AI", an H1 built on "Meet your AI copilot/teammate/agent", and an "Ask anything" chat box in place of a stated promise.
- **The brief pasted into copy** [3] (the "prompt language" in the rule above). Audience lines lifted from the brief ("for discerning buyers who value quality", "for teams of 10 to 200") read as prompt residue: no vendor describes its buyers by the brief's segment band. Name the audience the way it names itself, by role and context ("for site engineers on call", "for wedding photographers editing on location"). slop_check `--brief brief.txt` flags runs of 5+ words copied from the brief.
- **Design commentary in the source** [3]. A DESIGN.md pasted into an HTML comment ships the thesis, rejected directions and "values to confirm" to every visitor through view-source. Keep the spec in its own file or in the reply (slop_check `design-commentary`).
- **Slogan cadence** (synthesis of [4] "Not X. Just Y." and [2] full-stop statements). A parallel pair ("Made once. Kept forever.", "Built for life.") is a cadence, not a claim, unless it carries a fact no competitor can say. "No X, just Y" and its softened forms are the same formula. When a stock pattern is replaced (three tiers, FAQ, "Ready to...?"), delete its stock phrases too ("Every plan includes", "Most popular", "Get started").

Imagery:
- **Orbs, glows and gradient blobs instead of the product** [6]. Text plus a gradient blob is a placeholder, not a hero.
- **Generic stock** [3]: Unsplash placeholders, hands on laptops, smiling people, generic icon sets, generic generated graphics. Use your own product shots, commissioned photography or brand-specific illustration.
- **Flat vector art where a photo belongs** [2]. Tell the generator "no illustrations" and use real photos of the product or subject.
- **Faked product previews** [2]: placeholder dashboards (lorem rows, meaningless bars), fake version or "last sync" stamps, and typing animations of a fake prompt and answer. Show a screenshot of real output, a real component or editorial photography. A faithful HTML render of the product's real output (the actual commands, states and numbers, in the platform's chrome) is the real component, not this tell (synthesis of [6] and [2]).
- **Hero art from primitive SVG shapes** [1★]. Keep hand-built SVG for icons under about 64px and for charts. Use `clip-path` only for geometry (cut corners, diagonals, hexagons), and ship a cut-out raster or alpha matte for organic edges.

## 6. Microcopy and interface text

- **Label actions with the action** [4]. Use active voice and second person: "Save changes", not "Submit"; "Save API key", not "Continue"; "Install the CLI", not "The CLI will be installed". Confirmation buttons repeat the verb. Never use "OK", "Yes/No" or "Click here".
- **Real content before judging any design** [4]. Replace lorem ipsum and reference or AI-assumed copy with brief-specific names, numbers, product details and imagery. Lorem is acceptable only for checking visual weight. Re-derive spacing once the real content is in [3].
- **Errors say what happened and how to fix it** [3]: positive tone, no apology, no vagueness. After a submission, confirm the outcome item by item. On failure, name the specific cause (glare, blur, an expired key) and offer a retry or a manual path.
- **No "Oops!"** [3]. No exclamation marks in success messages, and no cute error copy.
- **Plain, user-side language** [2]. Name things by what users understand ("Manage notifications", not "Webhook config"). Describe rather than sell, use sentence case, and give each piece of text exactly one job.
- **Product UI headings are operational** [2]: "Analytics", "Plan status", "Last sync". No tagline, hero or aspirational subheading. Test: if a sentence could appear in an ad, rewrite it. Delete subheadings that only restate the page's purpose [1★].
- **Destinations, not riddles** [2]. Nav items name their destination, tab labels pair with an icon that reinforces them, and back buttons name the page they return to.
- **Help inline, at the point of need** [2]. Use visible inline text first and tooltips as a last resort.
- **Remove redundant labels** [2]. Merge a title and a repeated sub-label into one heading ("Your saved products"), drop labels whose values explain themselves, and use a logo instead of a text value where one exists.
- **One name per action through the whole flow** [1★] (Anthropic). A "Publish" button produces a "Published" toast, and the nouns stay identical everywhere.
- **Numbers** [1★] (Vercel). Use numerals for counts ("8 deployments"). Show currency with 0 or 2 decimals and never mix the two. Format dates and numbers with `Intl.DateTimeFormat`/`Intl.NumberFormat`, and put `translate="no"` on brand names and code.
- **Design every state and content extreme** [1★] (Vercel). Cover empty, sparse, dense and error states. Test short, average and very long strings with `truncate`, `line-clamp` or `break-words`, and give every screen a next step or a recovery path. Empty states distinguish first use, no results, filtered out, no permission and failure.
- **Ampersands** [1★] (Butterick). Spell out "and" in running text, and use "&" only in proper names (C14).
- **Primer before sensitive data** [1★]. Before asking for ID, payment or permissions, show why the data is needed, the steps involved, the time it takes ("About 2 minutes") and exactly how the data will be used.
- **Values the page needs but the brief lacks** (synthesis of [4] real content and [3] never inflate). Commercial facts (prices, dimensions, weight, delivery time, returns window, guarantee) are part of a product page, not invented proof: write plausible values and list each one as "to confirm" in the reply, never on the page. Proof is never invented: ratings, review counts, customer numbers, logos, quotes and outcome metrics are omitted until real.
- **Category-required trust facts** (synthesis). In B2B and regulated categories (finance, health, security, anything that writes to a system of record), buyers look for audit or security reports, data residency, encryption, uptime, privacy and terms. Leaving them out is a visible gap, not a neutral choice: list them as values to confirm and reserve a module and a nav or footer slot.

## 7. Distinctiveness: signature, motif, tests

- **Don't assemble the category's stock section sequence** [14]: logo cloud, stat strip, three feature cards, testimonial cards, three-tier pricing. The layout comes from this page's content.
- **Swap test** [4]. Every section must be specific to this business. If the first viewport or a layout could belong to a competitor, redesign it. One example hero is a biomarker range slider instead of a generic headline and image.
- **Squint test** [4]. Put the design next to products from unrelated categories. If you can't tell them apart, if the aesthetic could be guessed from the category alone, or if it matches a fashionable or AI look, rework it until the domain shows.
- **Repeat one proprietary motif sparingly** [4]. Take a shape, colour, curve or texture from the logo or the hero star and reuse it in a few places that already do a job: the primary button, link arrows, the loading state, image masks, a section background. Keep it to 3-4 places so it stays a signature, not wallpaper.
- **A signature that nobody sees is not one** (synthesis). Count a placement only if it is visible in the 390px and 1440px screenshots: uncropped, lettering at 12px or more and 3:1 against its ground, shapes clearly legible. At least one placement sits in the first viewport and at least one works at section scale (the page grid, a framing device), not only as a hairline detail. Every placement is the same visible form; a pictogram of the product category or a decorative bar that looks like a slider does not count. Test: from the first-viewport screenshot alone, someone not told the concept (or you, without DESIGN.md) can name the reference world.
- **Run the similar-prompt test on the signature itself** [5]. If a category leader's main screen already shows it (a candlestick chart in a trading app, a ring gauge in a fitness app), it is the category's vernacular. Push it with a property the leaders lack, taken from the reference world (the paper grid of a printed original, its handwritten annotations), or choose another.
- **Ration the signature effect** [3]. Put a motion-blur gradient, an offset shadow or a grid motif in the hero and on the few elements that deserve attention, never on every card.
- **Derive the star from the product's story** [2]. A data-to-dashboard tool gets a star that abstractly references a chart. Never pick a novelty skin (mirror ball, chrome, balloons) because it looks cool.
- **Borrow structure, add your own layer** [2]. Use an established structural style (linear, brutalist) as the layout constraint, then add your own visuals, texture and signature colour.
- **One deliberate imperfection** [2], such as a hand-drawn button with uneven edges or a symmetry broken at an awkward crossing. Refine it until it still holds together.

## 8. Marketing copy: specific, short, true

- **One idea per section** [3]: one short headline, at most one short paragraph, and one visual or one CTA. Only a few words should be visible at a time. One source caps this at ≤8 words for the headline and ≤25 for the paragraph (not consensus). For lists longer than 5 items, show the top 3-5 behind a "View full list", or use tabs or an accordion.
- **Never inflate** [3]. No made-up or identical stats, no overstated claims, no clickbait. Design must not make the product look more powerful or valuable than it is (Rams).
- **Concrete proof** [3]: named testimonials with real photos, real client logos, measured numbers, case studies, and for services a founder photo and bio. Every item must be verifiable and placed next to the claim it supports (C6).
- **Short confident benefit lines** [2]. A short declarative line about one benefit, in sentence case and ending with a full stop, reads as premium. Use it only when the category is already known (C5). Cap the form (synthesis): the hero plus at most 2 section heads; utility modules (specs, pricing, hours, integrations, security, care, FAQ) take plain labels, and three paragraphs in a row never open with the brand name (slop_check `heading-cadence`). Story and feature heads on a brand surface carry a fact, material or moment only this product has ("Eight hours on skin, smoke last"), in varied forms; the visitor's literal question ("What's in it", "How long does it last?") belongs in an FAQ, and a page of such heads reads as a department-store template (synthesis).
- **Let imagery carry visual showcases** [2]. Cut feature text down to a short headline or the product name; one redesign cut about 90% of the text. This doesn't apply to content pages or unknown brands (C7).
- **Don't strip useful information to look clean** [2]. Information-sparse luxury minimalism works only for brands everyone already knows.
- **Short brand story right after the hero** [2]: who you are, what you do, for whom, your speciality, values and process, backed by physical evidence (team, place, work).
- **Write to the motivation** [2]. Frame product pages around the life and feeling of owning the product, not only its attributes.
- **Copy before or with the visuals** [2]. Write the brand story, content plan and real copy first, then refine copy and journey together with the visuals. A good layout can't rescue weak copy.
- **Don't shout** [2]. No SALE or % OFF banners and no red-and-yellow promo callouts. Use at most one exclamation mark in a document longer than 3 pages.
- **Feature callouts** [1★] (Apple pattern). Open with the part's name in bold, then one plain sentence in regular weight and a muted colour.

## 9. Generated imagery, illustration and art direction

- **Direct before generating** [2]. Build a mood board, sketch or generate several options, then prompt with an explicit style, palette, composition and mood. Reuse supplied images before generating new ones.
- **Simple, unified forms** [2] (Prägnanz) for marks, icons and illustrations. People remember simple figures better.
- **Layer UI behind the subject** [2]. Large type or a clock can tuck behind the photo's subject, so the subject overlaps the UI and stays the focus. Use it once per site, as a signature move.
- **Props from the audience's world** [2]. A few cut-out objects (a skillet for restaurant software, a keyboard for freelancers) around the hero, or photos of real users' environments as section backgrounds.
- **Tilted or scattered images** [2], for editorial or campaign layouts only. Rotate each image deliberately (the example uses -20deg to 10deg on a `1fr 2fr 3fr 4fr` grid), never at random.
- **Diagrams in code** [1★] (Rauno). Build supporting diagrams and explainer visuals in HTML, CSS and SVG, with no libraries, so they stay crisp and reusable. Hero-scale organic art is still a raster (section 5).
- **Locked composition for sequences** [1★]. Image sets or clips that will animate or scroll-scrub together keep the same background colour, the same x/y position for the product and a locked-off camera.
- **Clean background plate** [1★]. Generate the art plate with all logos, nav and text removed, and give the coding agent the reference plus the plate so the UI is built in real code over a real image.
- **Specific historical sources beat AI clichés** [1★]. Collaged vintage scientific plates or archival ephemera read as handmade and ownable.

## 10. Social sales posts (photo-led)

**Scope and evidence** (synthesis). This section covers posts whose job is to sell one product with a photo, a price and an order channel (Instagram feed, WhatsApp status). Other posters and social graphics (event announcements, illustrated or typographic posters), packaging, identity and print stay out of scope: a separate graphic-design mode won 3 of 24 blind votes against no skill. Every rule below is synthesis from one test.
- **Material:** a Nigerian food brand's four 1080x1350 flyers, judged blind by model judges (agents given two lenses: a creative director and a customer scrolling on a phone).
- **Round 1:** a full-bleed photo with the copy on its own surface beat the brand's original illustrated flyers and a framed-photo variant in all 9 judgements (overall 7.3 vs 6.3 vs 3.1). The originals still led on offer clarity.
- **Round 2:** a revision adding the name, facts-line and order-bar rules below, all taken from round 1 judge notes, beat the round 1 winner in 8 of 9 (offer clarity 7.9 vs 5.8).

There was no no-skill baseline, so the test supports this layout over the originals, not a skill-vs-no-skill win. Treat it as directional.

**The layout that won** (after round 2)
- **One photo is the canvas.** A single art-directed shot fills the post: the product at its best (the dish served, not the bag). It sits in the upper half, and the lower 35-40% is clean, empty surface, so the copy sits on the photo's own tabletop. A soft gradient of that surface colour behind the copy holds 4.5:1 over any highlights. A framed photo between cream bands lost to it every time.
- **A range is one set.** Each product gets its own surface colour, taken from what it is made of (leaf green, sorghum red, cocoa brown, maize yellow), under one camera angle and one light, so the profile grid reads as a set.
- **The name buyers ask for is the display line** (about 110-132px on 1080; the test used 132px, shrunk to fit long names), with the brand's sentence as a lead under it. A product name hidden in a small tracked eyebrow vanished at phone size, and every round 2 judge flagged it.
- **One facts line, not a three-column bullet row.** The row read as a template, wrapped into orphans and repeated the headline.
- **A solid order bar:**
  - the channel icon with an action label ("WhatsApp or call to order")
  - the number at 64-70px in tabular figures
  - the price in the display face, on the number's baseline
  - the unit and the delivery line on one shared baseline
- **The logo sits on a calm patch of the photo.** No horizon line, prop or steam crosses it.

**Phone size.** Posts are seen about 390px wide (0.36x), so the skill's 12px floor means about 34px on a 1080 canvas. Labels, units and facts set at 18-26px were flagged by nearly every judge, and the final 29-31px lines were still called small. Aim for 34px or more, and check a 390px render.

**Rendering.** Render the fixed canvas in headless Chrome at 1080x1350 with device scale 2, and review a 390px-wide downscale. render_check.py's 320-1440 sweep and the web-only checklist items (scroll, targets, states) do not apply; slop_check.py still does.

**Offer completeness.** A price needs its unit. "Per pack" with no size drew a pack-size question from every judge of those flyers (typically "how many kg?"). Never invent pack sizes, packaging, claims or prices: ask, and show the real pack if the client supplies a photo.

**Generated food.**
- In this test the image model produced the wrong version of regional dishes: whole beans for gbegiri (a smooth bean purée), falafel-like patties for akara, mashed-potato texture for pap, chopped leaves for ewedu. Expect the same for other cultural objects (an extrapolation).
- Name the texture in the prompt ("completely smooth purée, no whole beans"), get reference photos from the client and check every item before layout.
- Patterned light (leaf, palm or blind shadows, golden-hour stripes) on a seamless sweep reads as stock AI light: use one soft, even window light with no pattern.
- Fix wrong food by regenerating it, not by retouching. Hand-built inpainting left seams and ghost grains that a full-resolution review caught.
- Keep all text out of the generated image; set the type in HTML/CSS and render at 2x.
- A generated serving shot shows what the product makes, never the product itself or its pack (C2).

## Context rules (conflict resolutions)

- **C1 Which image for which role.** Default: isolated cut-outs on a matched sweep for product, catalogue and detail views. Use one art-directed scene per section (product in use, the place, real people) for hero and campaign moments. Grade all of them as one set. For software, real UI screenshots replace product photography.
- **C2 Generated imagery.** Default: owned photography or real screenshots. Use generated images only for atmosphere or as comps before a shoot. Never use them to depict the actual product, real customers, results or the team. Replace them with owned photography before a premium launch. Exception: a social sales post (§10) may use a generated serving shot of what the product makes, checked against reference photos; never the product or pack itself.
- **C3 Hero structure.** Let the star decide. For a physical product or place with strong imagery, use a centred product-led hero, or a full-bleed image with overlaid text on an art-directed calm area with a verified scrim. For software or services, use a left-aligned text stack on the shared page edge with a real product visual. A split is fine once its template parts are gone (badge row, stat row, ghost "Watch demo") and its visual owns a full-height scene panel; a product floating on the page ground beside a text stack is the copy-left/image-right template (synthesis). Overlapping type and product is a once-per-site signature, not a default. Untreated text on a busy photo is the template look.
- **C4 Type or product as the biggest thing.** One star per view. A visually strong product is the largest element. Type becomes the star only when there is no strong visual (services, abstract software, a name reveal). Make type huge because it is the subject, never to fill space.
- **C5 Specific headline or short tagline.** For an unknown brand or product, specificity wins: the headline (or the headline plus one line) says what it is and what it achieves. Short evocative fragments only work when the visitor already knows the category (established brand, product named in an eyebrow), and even then they need a plain descriptive subline. Never use formula triads, "Not X. Just Y." or em-dash punchlines.
- **C6 Social proof or template strips.** Keep proof that is real and needed, and drop the template form. Every section must answer a question the visitor actually has. Logo walls suit B2B, founder and team proof suits services, and product pages lean on product facts. One strong quote with a real photo beats three testimonial cards.
- **C7 Imagery-led or text-led.** Imagery leads on visual product showcases and for known brands. Text is the primary content on articles, docs and any page people visit to read. Unknown businesses shorten their copy but keep the information.
- **C8 Atmosphere: gradients or real things.** Real things win: photography, lit renders, real texture, a flat tinted ground. Use a gradient only when something physical motivates it (light across a surface, a vignette seating a product), at hero scale and low contrast, never on buttons or text. Use grain at a few percent opacity on photo or flat fields only.
- **C9 Looping hero video.** Allowed only when the footage is the content (a place, the product in use): muted, with a poster frame, a pause control past 5s and a reduced-motion fallback. Never generic b-roll, and never added without earning its page weight.
- **C10 Scroll-scrubbed product reveals.** Default: no reveal, or one compressed video scrubbed by scroll (assets reported up to 80% smaller). Use a frame sequence only when per-frame control is the point, lazy-loaded, never more than one per page, with a single fallback image on slow connections.
- **C11 Colour and B&W, or collage.** Default: one grade and several clean images. Mix colour and B&W only as a systematic rule (for example, B&W for every portrait). Use a collage only as deliberate art direction, built from generated or archival layers.
- **C12 Identical or native.** The brand layer is identical everywhere: palette, type voice, imagery treatment, tone of voice, motion character. The behaviour layer is native to each platform: system controls, navigation, text styles and gestures.
- **C13 Trends, named worlds and timelessness.** Timelessness wins by default. Use a genre or "named world" look only when it is the product's own world (a game, a publication, a lab tool), not as a costume. "Uncommon" means specific to this brand, not new this season.
- **C14 Ornament or character.** The removal pass wins for decoration without information. Character comes from one committed main move carried by elements that already work: type, a real colour, art-directed imagery, scale. If the page then fails the swap test as a grey template, commit harder on an existing element instead of adding new ones.
- **C15 Conventional or distinctive.** Interaction, navigation, forms and controls stay conventional. Visual voice, imagery, typography, composition and copy are where distinctiveness belongs.
- **C16 Palette source.** Purple-on-dark and cream-terracotta-serif are both spent, so swapping one for the other fixes nothing. Derive the palette from the subject's world. Cream is valid only when the subject is made of it (paper, bakery, ceramics, hospitality). Always replace the terracotta/rust accent; default to a sans display. A serif display on cream needs a written reason and must be upright and outside the overused pool.
- **C17 Spot colour on a headline word.** No. Spot colour goes on the focal object or action. The exception is campaign art where the word is the subject (a colourway name): once per site, solid colour, never gradient text.
- **C18 Primary CTA colour.** Use the brand accent if the brand has a real one. Use near-black when imagery or product supplies the colour (fashion, luxury, photography-led). A black pill on an otherwise grey minimal page reads as a template. The secondary action is a text link by default.
- **C19 Light, dark or black stage.** Default to light. Use one inverted or black section for a pro or flagship render moment. Make the whole page dark only when the use scene argues for it (media tools, cinematic products, night use).
- **C20 Casing.** Default: sentence case for headings, buttons and labels, with normal letter-spacing. Title Case only where the platform convention requires it (macOS menus and buttons (std)), applied to that whole surface. Never mix the two within one surface.
- **C21 Ampersands.** Spell out "and" in sentences. "&" goes in proper names, plus space-constrained UI labels (tabs, nav) if used consistently.
- **C22 Brand-red or brand-green accents.** Semantic meaning wins. The accent can stay, but errors and success use a different shade plus an icon and text, and a red accent stays small (logo, CTA), never a dominant ground.

## Checks an agent can run

- [ ] Grep rendered text for `lorem|ipsum|John Doe|Acme|placeholder|TODO`: 0 hits.
- [ ] Grep visible copy (case-insensitive) for `elevate|seamless|unleash|next-gen|revolutioni[sz]e|game-?changer|delve|tapestry|streamline|empower|supercharge|world-class`: 0 hits.
- [ ] Count `—` (U+2014) in visible strings: 0. Count middle dots or pipes: at most 1 per line.
- [ ] No button or link text equals `Submit|Continue|OK|Yes|No|Click here|Learn more` without an object (a lone "Learn more" next to one specific product is fine).
- [ ] Every CTA label is 1-3 words, renders on one line at 375px wide (std), and the same intent uses the same words everywhere.
- [ ] Every success or error message has 0 exclamation marks and no "Oops". Each error string names a cause and a fix.
- [ ] Action verb consistency: each button verb has a matching past-tense toast (Publish → Published).
- [ ] H1 wraps to 2 lines or fewer at 1440px (std). Replace the brand name with a competitor's: if the H1 still reads true, rewrite it.
- [ ] The first viewport holds the brand, the H1, ≤1 supporting sentence, 1 primary CTA (+≤1 text link) and 1 dominant visual. No stat row, logo strip, pill eyebrow or "Introducing" label.
- [ ] (marketing, editorial, venue; not docs) Remove-the-image test: hide the hero image. If the first viewport still works, the image is too weak.
- [ ] No `images.unsplash.com`, `picsum`, `placehold`, initial-letter avatars, styled-text logos or empty `src` in production.
- [ ] Every `<img>` has `width`/`height`. Only above-fold media has `fetchpriority="high"`, and the rest has `loading="lazy"`. Decorative images have `alt=""`.
- [ ] Text over images: measure contrast over the actual image pixels behind the text box at mobile, tablet and desktop widths: ≥4.5:1 body, ≥3:1 large.
- [ ] Product cut-out backgrounds match the section background hex exactly, with no visible image edge in a screenshot.
- [ ] One grade: in a full-page screenshot, every photo shares colour temperature, contrast and lighting style. List and fix outliers.
- [ ] Videos: `muted`, `playsinline` and `poster` are present, there is a pause control if the loop exceeds 5s, and `prefers-reduced-motion` shows the poster.
- [ ] No placeholder dashboards or terminals and no typing-animation demos. Product visuals are real screenshots, real components or faithful renders of real output.
- [ ] Squint test and swap test on a screenshot next to two competitors and one unrelated product. If you can't tell them apart, fail.
- [ ] A spec file (DESIGN.md or tokens) exists with a personality paragraph, 1-3 target emotions, and named colour tokens with jobs. Components contain no hex outside it.
- [ ] Logo: reads at 16px and 32px, works in one flat colour, and has no glow or gradient. Your own logo is inline SVG.
- [ ] Brand motif appears in 3-4 functional places at most. The signature effect is absent from ordinary cards.
- [ ] Every signature placement is visible in the 390px and 1440px screenshots; one is in the first viewport; the reference world can be named from that screenshot alone.
- [ ] No visual asset appears more than twice or in adjacent sections; no fact (number plus unit) appears twice within a section (render_check `repeat-facts`).
- [ ] No phrase of 5+ words is copied from the brief; no design commentary in the shipped source; every value assumed for the brief is listed in the reply.
- [ ] With no logo supplied, the header and favicon use a drawn mark derived from the signature, and the wordmark is not styled like a link.
